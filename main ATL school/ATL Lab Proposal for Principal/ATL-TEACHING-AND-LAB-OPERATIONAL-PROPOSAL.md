## 1. Introduction

Respected Principal,

This proposal explains **how I will teach our students in the Atal Tinkering Lab (ATL)**, how the classroom is organized into working teams, and how this hands-on model directly benefits both our students and our school.

Rather than teaching theory on a blackboard, the lab is run as an **active hands-on workshop** where students build real working projects in teams.

---

## 2. Classroom Organization: The 5-Student Team Model

Our school has **400 students across Classes 5 to 9** (5 grades $\times$ 2 sections C and D = 10 batches of 40 students).

During each lab period, **one batch of 40 students** enters the lab. We organize the class into **8 teams of 5 students each**:

```
[ 40 Students in the Lab ] ───▶ Divided into 8 Working Tables (5 Students per Table)
```

### How the 5 Students Work Together in a Team:
In every project, each of the 5 students has a clear responsibility so that everyone is actively engaged:
1. **Mechanical Builder**: Assembles the chassis, wheels, brackets, and structural frame.
2. **Circuit & Wiring Lead**: Places components on the breadboard, connects jumper wires, and checks battery polarity.
3. **Programmer**: Operates the computer, writes the code, and uploads it to the microcontroller board.
4. **Sensor Specialist**: Adjusts and tests the sensors (light, distance, or infrared) to make sure they detect correctly.
5. **Tester & Logbook Lead**: Tests the completed project, records what worked or failed, and maintains the team project notebook.

*These roles rotate every project, ensuring all boys and girls learn hardware, wiring, coding, and problem-solving.*

## 3. What Each Grade Learns (Classes 5 to 9)

To keep learning exciting and age-appropriate, different classes build different projects:

* **Class 5 (Basic Electricity & Motion)**:
  - Students learn how circuits work using batteries, switches, small bulbs, and simple DC motors.
  - *Sample Project*: Motorized fan boat, vibrating art bot.
  - *Microcontroller*: None needed (pure hands-on basics).

* **Class 6 (Electronic Components & Breadboards)**:
  - Students build circuits on solderless breadboards using LEDs, resistors, capacitors, and 555 timers.
  - *Sample Project*: Dark-activated night lamp (LDR), police siren alarm.
  - *Microcontroller*: None needed (analog electronics).

* **Class 7 (First Steps in Smart Automation)**:
  - Students are introduced to the Arduino microcontroller board and learn how code controls physical parts.
  - *Sample Project*: Automatic touchless hand-sanitizer / smart dustbin (using an ultrasonic distance sensor and a micro-servo motor).

* **Class 8 (Robotics & Autonomous Machines)**:
  - Students build mobile robotics combining motors, motor driver shields, sensors, and programming.
  - *Sample Project*: **Autonomous Line-Following Robot Car**.

* **Class 9 (Internet of Things & Advanced Systems)**:
  - Students work with Wi-Fi microcontrollers (NodeMCU/ESP8266), mobile apps, and drone flight fundamentals.
  - *Sample Project*: Wi-Fi Weather Station showing live temperature on a phone dashboard, Educational Drone Assembly.

## 4. Project Example: How I Teach the "Line-Following Car" (Class 8)

To understand how a project is conducted from start to finish, here is the 4-week teaching plan for the **Line-Following Car**:

* **Week 1 — Mechanical Assembly**:
  The team builds the 2WD robot chassis, attaches the twin motors, rubber wheels, and front castor wheel.
* **Week 2 — Motor Driver & Wiring**:
  Students wire the motor driver board to the Arduino and test simple motion commands (forward, backward, turn).
* **Week 3 — Sensor Calibration**:
  Students attach the infrared (IR) tracking sensors and tune them so the robot detects black track lines on white floors.
* **Week 4 — Coding & Track Testing**:
  The programmer writes the logic: *“If left sensor sees black, turn left; if right sensor sees black, turn right.”* Teams run their cars on the lab track and fine-tune their speed.

### Smart Component Calculation for the School:
- **1 Car per Group of 5 students**.
- **8 Cars** needed for the active batch of 40 students.
- For both sections of Class 8 (Batch 8C + 8D = 16 groups), we only need **16 car kits + 2 teacher spares = 18 kits total**.
- **Savings for School**: After Class 8 finishes their project evaluation, the parts are dismantled and returned to storage for the next batch. We never need to purchase 80 or 400 robot kits.

## 5. How This Benefits Our Students

1. **Practical Problem Solving**: Students move from memorizing textbook theory to solving real physical problems with their own hands.
2. **Teamwork & Leadership**: Working in small groups of 5 teaches collaboration, communication, and technical leadership to both boys and girls equally.
3. **Future-Ready Skills**: Early exposure to coding, robotics, and electronics builds strong foundations for future engineering and technological careers.

---

## 6. How This Benefits Our School

1. **Maximum Budget Efficiency**: By calculating components for concurrent 40-student batches (8 teams) and using reusable kits, the school avoids buying excess inventory or wasting money on unused parts.
2. **High Institutional Reputation**: Working student robotics, automated models, and smart projects can be showcased at Science Exhibitions, inter-school STEM competitions, and Parent-Teacher meetings.
3. **Disciplined Lab Management**: Each team has a dedicated, labeled storage box for their parts, ensuring zero component loss, no clutter on tables, and complete student accountability.
