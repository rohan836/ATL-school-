# Questions That Help Students Think and Learn

Use questions to make students predict, reason, test, and explain. Avoid giving the answer immediately when a student encounters a problem.

## 1. Before building: prediction questions

- What do you think will happen when we connect the power?
- Which component do you think will control the output?
- What might happen if we reverse the LED?
- How could we make the light turn on only when it is dark?
- What do you think the sensor will report when the object moves farther away?
- Which part of the system is the input, and which part is the output?

Ask students to state a prediction and a reason—not just guess.

## 2. During building: reasoning questions

- Why did you choose this connection?
- What does this component do in the circuit?
- How does electricity get from the power source to the output?
- What would happen if this wire were disconnected?
- Which part should we test first?
- Can you explain the circuit using a drawing?
- What evidence shows that the sensor is working?

## 3. During troubleshooting: debugging questions

- Is the power source connected and suitable?
- Is the circuit path complete?
- Are the component legs in the correct breadboard rows?
- Is the LED or other polarized component facing the correct way?
- What did you change most recently?
- Can we test the input and output separately?
- What is one change we can make and then test?
- How do we know the change helped?

Encourage students to isolate one fault at a time rather than changing many wires simultaneously.

## 4. During testing: evidence questions

- What happens at different distances or light levels?
- Did the result match your prediction?
- What readings did you collect?
- Does the system behave consistently?
- What conditions make it fail?
- How could you make the test fair?
- What is the difference between one successful test and a reliable system?

## 5. During improvement: engineering questions

- What is the biggest weakness of your current design?
- Which change could improve reliability?
- Can you make it use fewer components?
- Can you make it easier for another student to operate?
- What happens when the environment changes?
- How could you improve the design without changing its main purpose?

## 6. During presentation: explanation questions

- What problem does your project solve?
- What is the input, processing, and output?
- Which concept did you learn while building?
- What was the most difficult fault to find?
- What evidence shows that the project works?
- What would you change if you had more time?
- What is one limitation of your design?

## 7. Teacher questioning routine

A simple routine for any practical task:

1. **Ask:** “What do you think will happen?”
2. **Wait:** Give students time to think or discuss.
3. **Probe:** “Why do you think that?”
4. **Test:** Let students use the circuit or measurement to check.
5. **Reflect:** “What did the result teach us?”

Do not use questions only to check memory. Use them to help students develop engineering reasoning.
