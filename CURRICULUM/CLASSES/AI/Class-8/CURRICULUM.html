Duration: 6 months · 12 sessions per month · 72 sessions total. Core period: 60 minutes per session; optional 15-minute extension. Sections: C and D share this curriculum; this is the single common copy.

Course goal

Apply modular Python and data analysis to baseline ML, evaluation, neural-network concepts and small source-grounded retrieval systems.

Learning outcomes

  • Write modular Python with files, exceptions and tests.
  • Clean, explore and visualise modest datasets.
  • Compare baselines and ML results; interpret a confusion matrix and errors.
  • Explain tokens, embeddings, attention and retrieval at a conceptual level.

Month-by-month project briefs

Month 1 — Python engineering foundations

Concepts: Functions, lists/dictionaries, files, exceptions, modules, readable code and tests.

Build: Create a small file-backed utility. Separate functions, validate input, handle an error and test multiple cases.

Evidence: project artefact, test record, explanation and one limitation. Use paper/offline alternatives where needed.

Month 2 — Data analysis

Concepts: CSV, data cleaning, transformations, summaries, charts and notebook reproducibility.

Build: Document columns and source, clean missing/duplicate/inconsistent values, chart results and keep the analysis reproducible.

Evidence: project artefact, test record, explanation and one limitation. Use paper/offline alternatives where needed.

Month 3 — Machine-learning workflow

Concepts: Baseline, features/labels, train/test split, fitting and data leakage intuition.

Build: Define a small classification/regression task, establish a baseline and compare a beginner model or paper simulation on held-out examples.

Evidence: project artefact, test record, explanation and one limitation. Use paper/offline alternatives where needed.

Month 4 — Evaluation and behaviour

Concepts: Confusion matrix, accuracy, precision/recall concepts, overfitting, generalisation and error analysis.

Build: Build a confusion matrix from results, inspect errors and explain why a single accuracy value may hide important weaknesses.

Evidence: project artefact, test record, explanation and one limitation. Use paper/offline alternatives where needed.

Month 5 — Neural networks and modern AI

Concepts: Weights/learning intuition, tokens, embeddings, attention concept, retrieval-augmented generation.

Build: Search a small approved text collection and answer using retrieved passages. Show evidence and say when the answer is not in the source.

Evidence: project artefact, test record, explanation and one limitation. Use paper/offline alternatives where needed.

Month 6 — Applied capstone

Concepts: Problem scope, reproducibility, evaluation, documentation, safety and human review.

Build: Create a modest data/AI prototype with baseline, tests, error-driven revision, source note and risk review. GPU/cloud is not required.

Evidence: project artefact, test record, explanation and one limitation. Use paper/offline alternatives where needed.

Assessment and teacher guide

Formative assessment

Use prediction questions, concept checks, code/algorithm tracing, test logs, observation and short exit tickets. Evaluate understanding—not speed, polish, device access or whether a model always gives a correct answer.

Capstone rubric (20 points)

Score each category 0–4: problem/plan; understanding; build and individual contribution; testing and evidence-based improvement; responsible use and communication. A 0 means no evidence yet; 2 means developing with partial evidence; 4 means clear, accurate and independently justified evidence. Use the rubric formatively and adapt for accessibility.

Minimum project evidence

  • A clear problem and chosen approach.
  • Program, notebook, physical artefact or explicitly labelled paper simulation.
  • Test cases/results appropriate to the grade.
  • One issue or limitation identified and one change made because of evidence.
  • A short explanation of privacy, fairness or reliability, including where a person checks the result.
  • Each learner explains their own contribution.

60-minute lesson rhythm

5 min retrieval/challenge · 10 min explanation/demo · 25 min build/investigation · 10 min testing/debugging · 10 min explanation/exit ticket. Adjust pace; do not skip testing or safety to finish a build. An optional 15-minute extension may support additional practice or stretch work.

Differentiation

Support: starter templates, worked examples, fewer features, sentence frames, paired tracing. Core: build/modify, test multiple cases, record and fix an issue. Extension: compare approaches, add edge cases or justify a metric. Accept spoken, written, diagram-based and accessible demonstrations.

Safety and responsible AI

Use school-approved tools and teacher-prepared, fictional, public or approved data. Never upload identifiable student names, faces, voices, locations, records or private work to public AI tools. Do not use models to classify real students by sensitive traits or make consequential decisions. Check factual claims against approved sources. No GPU, paid account, webcam, cloud service or public AI account is a prerequisite. Provide paper/offline alternatives. Keep any tool use transparent, permission-limited and under human supervision.

Teacher preparation

Check devices, software, permissions and connectivity; prepare a known-good example and an offline alternative. Ask students to predict, test unusual inputs, record errors and explain changes. Follow school safeguarding, privacy, copyright and acceptable-use policies.

Student reflection

What problem did we solve? How does it work? What did tests show? What failed or surprised us? What did we change and why? What should users not assume? What data/permissions need protection? Where must a person review the result?

Grade-specific implementation guidance

Technical expectations

Use small, teacher-approved datasets and standard local tools. Optional libraries may be introduced when available; activities must also work with CSV files and prepared outputs. GPU use and large neural-network training are not required. Restrict retrieval and tool demonstrations to an approved corpus and transparent tools.

Project pathway

Examples include classifying fictional environmental samples, predicting from a small classroom dataset, analysing public weather or school-garden data, or answering questions from teacher-approved documents while citing source text.

Teaching and safety

Keep experiments comparable. Record data source, features, test method, outcome, and a limitation. Separate training and test examples. Explain that sample accuracy does not guarantee real-world reliability. Protect sensitive student information, avoid consequential decisions about real people, and check AI answers against sources.

Success standard

A strong project has a baseline, appropriate test split and metric, error analysis, reproducible steps, and a clear statement of limits.
