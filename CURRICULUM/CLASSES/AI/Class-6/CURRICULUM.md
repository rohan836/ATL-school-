# Class 6 — AI & Computing Curriculum

**Duration:** 6 months · 12 sessions per month · 72 sessions total. **Core period:** 60 minutes per session; optional 15-minute extension. **Sections:** C and D share this curriculum; this is the single common copy.

## Course goal
Move from digital systems and algorithms into foundational Python, data visualisation and introductory machine-learning reasoning.

## Learning outcomes
- Explain CPU, RAM and storage at an introductory level.
- Use pseudocode/flowcharts and debug algorithms.
- Write Python with variables, conditions, loops, lists and simple functions.
- Interpret a chart and explain features, labels and prediction errors.

## Month-by-month project briefs

### Month 1 — Computer systems and digital practice
**Concepts:** Input/output; CPU, RAM and storage; operating-system basics; files and safe accounts.

**Build:** Map a familiar task through input, processing, output and storage. Sort examples of hardware/software and organise project files.

**Evidence:** project artefact, test record, explanation and one limitation. Use paper/offline alternatives where needed.

### Month 2 — Algorithms and problem solving
**Concepts:** Decomposition, pseudocode, flowcharts, selection, repetition and tracing.

**Build:** Describe a task in pseudocode/flowchart, trace it with sample inputs, test an edge case and debug unclear steps.

**Evidence:** project artefact, test record, explanation and one limitation. Use paper/offline alternatives where needed.

### Month 3 — Python foundations
**Concepts:** Variables, strings/numbers/booleans, input/output, conditions, loops and lists.

**Build:** Modify a teacher-tested quiz, timer simulation or choice story. Trace variables and test normal and unexpected inputs.

**Evidence:** project artefact, test record, explanation and one limitation. Use paper/offline alternatives where needed.

### Month 4 — Data and visualisation
**Concepts:** Rows/columns, categories, missing/inconsistent values, counts and simple charts.

**Build:** Inspect a small fictional or approved anonymous dataset, clean simple inconsistencies, chart counts and explain what the sample can and cannot show.

**Evidence:** project artefact, test record, explanation and one limitation. Use paper/offline alternatives where needed.

### Month 5 — Machine-learning basics
**Concepts:** Rules vs models; features, labels, classification, separate test examples and errors.

**Build:** Use fictional cards to classify by visible features. Set aside test examples, count mistakes and discuss how examples shape predictions.

**Evidence:** project artefact, test record, explanation and one limitation. Use paper/offline alternatives where needed.

### Month 6 — Build and evaluate
**Concepts:** Problem scoping, small Python/data prototype, testing, iteration and responsible use.

**Build:** Build a revision quiz, checklist, fictional library organiser or chart generator. Submit three tests, an improvement and a limitation.

**Evidence:** project artefact, test record, explanation and one limitation. Use paper/offline alternatives where needed.

## Assessment and teacher guide

### Formative assessment
Use prediction questions, concept checks, code/algorithm tracing, test logs, observation and short exit tickets. Evaluate understanding—not speed, polish, device access or whether a model always gives a correct answer.

### Capstone rubric (20 points)
Score each category 0–4: problem/plan; understanding; build and individual contribution; testing and evidence-based improvement; responsible use and communication. A 0 means no evidence yet; 2 means developing with partial evidence; 4 means clear, accurate and independently justified evidence. Use the rubric formatively and adapt for accessibility.

### Minimum project evidence
- A clear problem and chosen approach.
- Program, notebook, physical artefact or explicitly labelled paper simulation.
- Test cases/results appropriate to the grade.
- One issue or limitation identified and one change made because of evidence.
- A short explanation of privacy, fairness or reliability, including where a person checks the result.
- Each learner explains their own contribution.

### 60-minute lesson rhythm
5 min retrieval/challenge · 10 min explanation/demo · 25 min build/investigation · 10 min testing/debugging · 10 min explanation/exit ticket. Adjust pace; do not skip testing or safety to finish a build. An optional 15-minute extension may support additional practice or stretch work.

### Differentiation
Support: starter templates, worked examples, fewer features, sentence frames, paired tracing. Core: build/modify, test multiple cases, record and fix an issue. Extension: compare approaches, add edge cases or justify a metric. Accept spoken, written, diagram-based and accessible demonstrations.

### Safety and responsible AI
Use school-approved tools and teacher-prepared, fictional, public or approved data. Never upload identifiable student names, faces, voices, locations, records or private work to public AI tools. Do not use models to classify real students by sensitive traits or make consequential decisions. Check factual claims against approved sources. No GPU, paid account, webcam, cloud service or public AI account is a prerequisite. Provide paper/offline alternatives. Keep any tool use transparent, permission-limited and under human supervision.

### Teacher preparation
Check devices, software, permissions and connectivity; prepare a known-good example and an offline alternative. Ask students to predict, test unusual inputs, record errors and explain changes. Follow school safeguarding, privacy, copyright and acceptable-use policies.

### Student reflection
What problem did we solve? How does it work? What did tests show? What failed or surprised us? What did we change and why? What should users not assume? What data/permissions need protection? Where must a person review the result?

## Grade-specific implementation guidance

### Progression and boundaries
Introduce loops before asking students to build multi-step programs; use small datasets before charts or classification. Students may use a prepared notebook, offline editor, or teacher-controlled tool. Do not require cloud compute, paid tools, webcam data, or training a large model. Generation tools are optional demonstrations, not the course's technical foundation.

### Project pathway
Suggested projects include a quiz with conditions, a simple study timer or checklist, a survey-summary chart, or a paper/teacher-guided classifier using fictional data.

### Teaching and safety
Demonstrate first, ask students to predict, let them modify one thing at a time, test normal and unusual inputs, and have them explain their reasoning. Use fictional or teacher-prepared data. Do not upload identifiable student information, photos, recordings, or school records to external AI services. Verify important claims against trusted sources and keep a human responsible for decisions.

### Success standard
Assess working logic, explanation, test evidence, iteration, and responsible choices. A small program a student can explain is stronger evidence than copied code they cannot describe.
