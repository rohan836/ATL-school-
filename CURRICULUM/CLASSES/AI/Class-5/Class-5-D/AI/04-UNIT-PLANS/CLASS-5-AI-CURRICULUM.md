# Class 5 — AI-Only Curriculum

**Status:** Proposed curriculum for planning and review  
**Audience:** Typical Class 5 learners (approximately 10–11 years old)  
**Scope:** Artificial intelligence literacy only. No electronics, robotics builds, hardware, or coding prerequisite.  
**Structure:** 20 content units, ordered from foundations to advanced-for-Class-5 application. No fixed dates or weeks.

## Purpose

Students should learn what AI is, how AI systems learn patterns from examples, how generative AI differs from other AI, how to question AI outputs, and how to use or design AI responsibly. The goal is informed understanding—not simply learning to operate a chatbot or memorising AI vocabulary.

A unit can take one or more class periods. Teach through short explanations, paper-based activities, discussion, examples, and reflection. A teacher may demonstrate a vetted AI tool when school policy permits, but students do not need personal AI accounts or public AI access to complete this curriculum.

## Learning outcomes by the end of the course

Students should be able to:

- Explain AI in their own words and distinguish examples of AI from ordinary software or fixed-rule automation.
- Describe a simple AI process: examples and data → training → model → output → human checking.
- Explain why the examples used to train an AI system affect its results.
- Distinguish AI that classifies or predicts from generative AI that creates content.
- Write and improve a clear prompt, then assess the output rather than accepting it blindly.
- Identify plausible AI errors, verify factual claims, and discuss synthetic media carefully.
- Apply basic rules for privacy, consent, fairness, attribution, and responsible classroom use.
- Propose an AI solution to a suitable problem and explain how it should be tested and kept safe.

## The 20 units

### Stage 1 — Foundation: Understand AI (Units 1–4)

#### 1. AI Around Us
**Learn:** What artificial intelligence means; everyday examples such as translation, recommendations, image sorting, voice assistants, and generative tools. Not every digital feature is AI.

**Activity:** Sort example cards into “likely AI”, “ordinary software / fixed rule”, and “not enough information”. Students explain one choice instead of guessing from the product name.

**Evidence of learning:** Each student explains one example and one non-example in their own words.

#### 2. AI, Automation, and Ordinary Software
**Learn:** A fixed rule follows instructions written by people; some AI systems learn patterns from examples. A tool can combine both. “Automatic” does not always mean “AI”.

**Activity:** Compare a timer that rings after ten minutes, a rule-based quiz, and a system that learns to sort sample pictures. Identify what each one uses to make a decision.

**Evidence of learning:** Students give a reason for classifying each example.

#### 3. What AI Can—and Cannot—Do
**Learn:** AI can perform specific tasks well, but it can make mistakes and miss context. AI output does not prove that a system has human feelings, intentions, experience, or judgement.

**Activity:** Give groups a set of tasks—checking a spelling, comforting a friend, estimating tomorrow’s weather, deciding whether a claim is fair. Discuss where AI might help and where a person must lead.

**Evidence of learning:** Students name one useful AI capability and one limitation without describing AI as a person.

#### 4. Inside an AI System
**Learn:** A simple model of an AI system: input → data/examples → training → model → output → human review. Different AI systems use different designs; this is a useful overview, not a universal technical recipe.

**Activity:** Arrange process cards in order and trace a pretend picture-sorting system from its examples to a prediction. Add a “check the result” step.

**Evidence of learning:** Students explain the process using the diagram and identify where a mistake might enter.

### Stage 2 — Beginner: Data and Machine Learning (Units 5–9)

#### 5. Data: Examples That Teach an AI
**Learn:** Data can be pictures, words, sounds, measurements, or other recorded information. Training examples should be relevant, varied, and sufficiently accurate.

**Activity:** Review a fictional set of animal or fruit cards. Discuss what the set includes, what it leaves out, and how missing examples could affect a classifier.

**Evidence of learning:** Students identify two qualities of a useful example set and one problem caused by poor data.

#### 6. Features, Labels, and Categories
**Learn:** A feature is a useful clue or property; a label is the category or answer attached to an example. The clues selected can change the result.

**Activity:** Make a paper dataset for sorting fictional leaves, fruit, or animals. Choose visible features and labels, then check whether the same clues work for every card.

**Evidence of learning:** Students correctly identify a feature and a label in a small dataset and explain one limitation of their choices.

#### 7. Classification: Grouping New Examples
**Learn:** Classification assigns an input to a category, such as identifying the likely type of an animal in a picture. Predictions can be wrong, especially when examples are unclear or unusual.

**Activity:** Use example cards to develop a simple category system, then give students unfamiliar cards to classify. Compare their decisions and discuss ambiguous cases.

**Evidence of learning:** Students explain the predicted category and the clues used, while acknowledging uncertainty when the clues are insufficient.

#### 8. Training by Examples: Build a Paper Classifier
**Learn:** Training means using examples to help a model learn patterns. A paper activity can simulate parts of this idea, but a human-made card game is not itself a trained AI model.

**Activity:** Teams receive a small, fictional training set and design a pretend classifier using selected clues. They record which examples guided their design and what they expect the classifier to do.

**Evidence of learning:** Teams show their examples, chosen clues, and expected categories. They can explain what their paper simulation leaves out about real AI.

#### 9. Testing on New Examples
**Learn:** A system must be checked on examples it did not use during training. Accuracy describes how often predictions are correct in a defined test set; mistakes reveal limits. Memorising familiar examples is not the same as handling new ones well.

**Activity:** Test the paper classifier from Unit 8 on a separate set of unseen cards. Record correct and incorrect predictions, then revise the model and test again.

**Evidence of learning:** Students report the result from their test set, give one example of an error, and explain why testing only familiar examples would be misleading.

### Stage 3 — Intermediate: Generative AI and Prompting (Units 10–13)

#### 10. AI That Recognises vs AI That Creates
**Learn:** Some AI systems classify, detect, translate, recommend, or predict; generative AI produces new text, images, audio, or other content. Creating something plausible is not the same as proving it is true.

**Activity:** Sort sample outputs by task: classify, predict/recommend, translate, or generate. Discuss cases where one tool may do more than one task.

**Evidence of learning:** Students distinguish generative output from a category prediction and give one reason generated content needs review.

#### 11. How Chatbots Produce Answers
**Learn:** Language models learn patterns from large amounts of text and generate likely continuations or responses. A fluent answer may still be inaccurate, incomplete, or made up; fluent language is not evidence of truth.

**Activity:** Complete a classroom “next-word” pattern game using a short, teacher-written sentence set. Compare how different examples change likely continuations, then discuss why this simplification is not a full explanation of a modern chatbot.

**Evidence of learning:** Students explain why a chatbot can sound convincing and still be wrong.

#### 12. Writing a Clear Prompt
**Learn:** A useful prompt states the task, gives relevant context, sets limits, and describes the desired format. A prompt improves direction but cannot guarantee correctness or safety.

**Activity:** Improve a vague request such as “Tell me about space” by adding a purpose, a Class 5 reading level, a word limit, and a format. Use fictional, non-personal examples only.

**Evidence of learning:** Students write a clear prompt and identify its task, context, limits, and requested format.

#### 13. Improve, Compare, and Edit AI Output
**Learn:** Prompting can be iterative. Comparing responses, asking a more precise question, and editing the result are useful skills; the student remains responsible for the final work.

**Activity:** Compare two teacher-prepared responses to the same fictional task. Score them against criteria such as relevance, clarity, age suitability, and factual support. Rewrite the better response in the students’ own words.

**Evidence of learning:** Students give a reason for their choice, make a purposeful revision, and identify what still needs checking.

### Stage 4 — Critical AI Literacy: Trust, Safety, and Fairness (Units 14–18)

#### 14. AI Mistakes and Fact-Checking
**Learn:** AI can provide incorrect facts, invented details, or answers that sound certain without enough evidence. Students should verify important claims using trusted sources and a teacher—not rely on confident wording.

**Activity:** Review a teacher-prepared answer containing a mixture of checkable facts and one planted error. Mark each statement as supported, unsupported, or still needing investigation; verify against a textbook or trusted reference.

**Evidence of learning:** Students use a simple process: identify the claim, check a reliable source, and correct or qualify the answer.

#### 15. AI-Generated Media and Deepfakes
**Learn:** Images, voices, video, and text can be generated or altered. A single visual clue is not a reliable way to prove media is fake or real; source, context, corroboration, and trusted adult support matter.

**Activity:** Compare clearly labelled synthetic and real example materials selected by the teacher. Practise asking: Who shared this? Where did it first appear? Is there independent confirmation? Avoid using images or voices of classmates.

**Evidence of learning:** Students explain why they should pause and verify suspicious content rather than immediately believe or forward it.

#### 16. Privacy, Consent, and Personal Data
**Learn:** Personal information may include a name, face, voice, location, contact details, password, school record, or private work. Students should not upload personal or identifying information to AI tools without clear school approval and adult guidance.

**Activity:** Sort fictional scenarios into “safe to share”, “ask a trusted adult first”, and “do not share”. Explain why consent and data minimisation matter.

**Evidence of learning:** Students state a practical privacy rule and apply it to an example involving an AI tool.

#### 17. Bias, Fairness, and Representation
**Learn:** If training examples are incomplete, unbalanced, or reflect unfair assumptions, a model may work less well for some people or situations. Fairness requires examining who is represented, who may be missed, and who is affected.

**Activity:** Test a fictional category system built from examples showing only one kind of object or situation. Ask who or what the examples leave out and how that may change predictions. Avoid asking students to label classmates by sensitive traits.

**Evidence of learning:** Students identify a possible source of bias and propose a specific improvement to the examples or evaluation.

#### 18. Responsible Use, Credit, and Original Work
**Learn:** AI can support brainstorming or explanation, but school rules still apply. Students should not present AI-generated work as entirely their own when it is not; follow teacher instructions for disclosure, respect other people’s work, and check content before using it.

**Activity:** Discuss fictional schoolwork scenarios: using AI to explain a difficult word, copying a generated essay, editing a paragraph, or submitting unverified facts. Decide which uses are responsible and explain why.

**Evidence of learning:** Students describe a responsible way to use AI as a learning aid and how to acknowledge help when required.

### Stage 5 — Advanced for Class 5: Impact and Design (Units 19–20)

#### 19. AI, People, and the Public Good
**Learn:** AI can help in areas such as accessibility, language support, science, agriculture, and environmental research, but it can also create errors, exclude people, consume resources, or be used harmfully. Not every problem needs AI.

**Activity:** Choose a fictional community problem. Compare three options: solve it without AI, use an ordinary fixed rule, or consider an AI approach. For each, discuss who benefits, who could be harmed, what evidence is needed, and where a human must stay involved.

**Evidence of learning:** Students justify whether AI is suitable for the chosen problem and identify one benefit, one risk, and one human responsibility.

#### 20. Capstone: Design and Evaluate an AI Idea
**Learn:** A responsible AI idea starts with a real need, considers examples and data, predicts likely mistakes, protects people, and includes a way to test results. A paper design is a proposal—not a claim that a working AI system has been built.

**Activity:** In a team, design a paper prototype for a suitable low-risk problem. The presentation must include:
1. The problem and who it helps.
2. Why AI is or is not a sensible approach.
3. The proposed input, examples/data, and intended output.
4. Three fictional test cases, including one difficult case.
5. A possible error or bias and how to check for it.
6. Privacy safeguards and where a person reviews the result.
7. How the team would measure whether the idea works.

**Evidence of learning:** Teams present their design, explain its limits, and respond to feedback. Do not collect classmates’ personal data or claim the paper prototype is a deployed AI.

## Assessment plan

Assess understanding and reasoning—not how many AI tools students can use.

- **After Unit 4:** Students draw and explain the simple AI process.
- **After Unit 9:** Students record test outcomes and explain a classifier’s mistake.
- **After Unit 13:** Students improve a prompt and critique an output against clear criteria.
- **After Unit 18:** Students solve a short privacy, fairness, or academic-integrity scenario and justify their choice.
- **Unit 20 capstone:** Use the 10-point rubric below.

### Capstone rubric (10 points)

Score each criterion from 0 to 2.

| Criterion | 0 points | 1 point | 2 points |
|---|---|---|---|
| Problem and AI fit | Problem unclear; no reason for AI | Problem stated; fit partly explained | Clear problem; sensible case for or against AI |
| Examples and testing | No usable examples or tests | Some examples and a basic test | Relevant fictional examples, including a difficult test |
| Evaluation | No way to judge results | Simple success check | Clear measure and explanation of likely errors |
| Safety and fairness | Risks ignored | One risk identified | Privacy, fairness, and human review considered |
| Explanation | Cannot explain the design | Partial explanation | Clear explanation, evidence, and honest limits |

## Safety and teaching boundaries

- No student accounts on public generative-AI services are required for this course. Any live demonstration must use a school-approved, teacher-controlled tool and comply with school policy.
- Never upload student names, faces, voices, locations, contact details, passwords, school records, or identifiable student work to an AI service for these lessons.
- Do not create or share deceptive, humiliating, or impersonating content involving real students or staff.
- Do not use AI output as the sole source for factual claims or as the sole decision-maker for consequential matters.
- Use fictional datasets and preselected media examples. Offer paper-based alternatives so access to a device is not a prerequisite.
- Teach that AI can assist people; it does not remove human responsibility.

## Research basis and design choices

This is a new, parallel **AI-only** curriculum. It is not copied from the repository’s existing electronics-linked curriculum and is not presented as an official CBSE syllabus.

1. **AI4K12 grade-band guidelines** structure K–12 AI around perception; representation and reasoning; learning; natural interaction; and societal impact. The units cover these ideas at an introductory level suitable for the Grade 3–5 band.
2. **UNESCO’s AI Competency Framework for Students** uses the progression Understand, Apply, and Create across human-centred thinking, AI ethics, AI techniques/applications, and AI system design. This plan follows that progression from basic recognition to testing and designing a responsible idea.
3. **CBSE’s 2026–27 Class 5 CT&AI Student Handbook** promotes activity-based, collaborative learning, reasoning, and formative assessment. It is an integrated computational-thinking-and-AI resource, so this separate plan uses its age-appropriate teaching approach without treating its contents as a standalone AI syllabus.
4. **Common Sense Education’s K–12 AI literacy lessons** emphasise understanding AI, considering benefits and risks, and responsible and ethical use. This informs the critical-literacy units and the use of teacher-selected examples.
