# Class 6 — AI-Only Curriculum

**Status:** Proposed curriculum for planning and review  
**Audience:** Typical Class 6 learners (approximately 11–12)  
**Scope:** Artificial intelligence literacy only. No electronics, robotics builds, or hardware. No coding prerequisite.  
**Progression:** 20 units from foundational concepts to advanced topics for this class. No fixed dates or weeks.

## Purpose

Students move beyond recognising AI: they explain how data and learned patterns influence predictions, test simple paper models, use generative AI critically, and apply privacy and fairness rules.

## Learning outcomes

- Explain a simple AI lifecycle and distinguish learned patterns from fixed rules.
- Describe data, features, labels, classification, training and testing using an example.
- Compare recommendation, classification and generative AI tasks.
- Improve a prompt and critique, verify and responsibly use a generated answer.
- Identify basic privacy, fairness, consent and misinformation risks.
- Design a paper-based AI idea with fictional examples, a test plan and a human review step.

## Stage 1 — Connect and Explain AI (Units 1–4)

### Unit 1. AI Systems Around Us
**Learn:** AI applications in search, translation, recommendations, image classification, accessibility and generative tools. The product name alone does not prove a feature uses AI.

**Activity:** Make an “AI / fixed rule / unsure” card sort and justify one classification.

**Evidence of learning:** A short explanation of one AI example and one non-example.

### Unit 2. AI, Automation and Fixed Rules
**Learn:** Fixed instructions, decision rules and learned models can behave similarly on easy cases but are built differently; real products may combine them.

**Activity:** Compare a timer, a rule-based quiz and a pretend learned picture classifier.

**Evidence of learning:** A comparison chart explaining how each system makes its decision.

### Unit 3. The AI Lifecycle
**Learn:** A simplified flow: define a task → select examples/data → train or configure a model → produce an output → evaluate → revise and monitor.

**Activity:** Arrange lifecycle cards and point out where bad data or unchecked outputs cause problems.

**Evidence of learning:** A labelled flow diagram with at least two failure points.

### Unit 4. Inputs, Patterns and Representations
**Learn:** AI works with encoded inputs such as text, images or measurements; features or patterns make some information useful for a task.

**Activity:** Describe how a picture could be represented by clues such as colour, shape and edges without claiming that a machine sees exactly like a person.

**Evidence of learning:** Students distinguish raw input from selected clues and an output.

## Stage 2 — Understand Machine Learning (Units 5–9)

### Unit 5. Data Quality and Labels
**Learn:** Examples need suitable labels, consistency and variety; missing or incorrect labels can mislead a model.

**Activity:** Audit a small fictional dataset of animal or plant cards for missing categories, duplicates and unclear labels.

**Evidence of learning:** A corrected sample dataset plus a reason for each correction.

### Unit 6. Features and Useful Clues
**Learn:** Features are properties used to make a prediction. Poorly chosen clues may work on familiar examples and fail elsewhere.

**Activity:** Choose features for a paper task such as classifying fictional leaves, then try unusual examples.

**Evidence of learning:** A feature list with one reasoned limitation.

### Unit 7. Classification and Prediction
**Learn:** Classification chooses a category; a prediction is an estimate and can be uncertain or wrong. Categories should fit the real task.

**Activity:** Classify previously unseen cards and mark examples where the evidence is ambiguous.

**Evidence of learning:** Predicted categories with evidence and a note for ambiguous cases.

### Unit 8. Training a Paper Model
**Learn:** A model learns patterns from examples; a classroom card rule can illustrate an idea but does not reproduce all the mechanisms of a real trained model.

**Activity:** Teams make a paper classifier using selected clues and explain which examples influenced its design.

**Evidence of learning:** A documented model rule and a statement of what the simulation leaves out.

### Unit 9. Test Data, Accuracy and Errors
**Learn:** Evaluate on new examples, not just those used to build the model. Accuracy is the proportion correct on a particular test set and does not describe every kind of error.

**Activity:** Use a separate test card set, tally results, inspect mistakes and revise once.

**Evidence of learning:** A test record with correct/incorrect totals and one limitation.

## Stage 3 — Explore Generative AI (Units 10–14)

### Unit 10. Classification, Recommendations and Generation
**Learn:** Classification assigns a category; recommendation ranks options; generative AI creates content. One product may combine different task types.

**Activity:** Match sample scenarios and outputs to task types.

**Evidence of learning:** Correctly classify four cases and justify a tricky example.

### Unit 11. How Text-Generating AI Works
**Learn:** A language model learns patterns in text and generates likely continuations. Fluent wording does not prove understanding or truth.

**Activity:** Use a short teacher-written next-word activity to explore how context changes likely continuations, then discuss its limits as a model of modern AI.

**Evidence of learning:** A simple explanation of why a fluent output can still be wrong.

### Unit 12. Prompt Design
**Learn:** A clear prompt specifies the task, context, intended audience, constraints and desired format; prompts guide an output but cannot guarantee correctness.

**Activity:** Improve a vague prompt by naming the task, Class 6 reading level, limits and output format.

**Evidence of learning:** A prompt with its task, context, constraints and format identified.

### Unit 13. Compare and Revise Outputs
**Learn:** Useful review criteria include relevance, clarity, completeness, factual support and suitability for the audience. Human editing remains important.

**Activity:** Evaluate two teacher-prepared responses against a shared checklist, then revise one.

**Evidence of learning:** A scored comparison and a purposeful revision.

### Unit 14. Fact-Checking Generated Answers
**Learn:** AI may invent details, omit context or present uncertain claims confidently. Important claims need independent checking against trustworthy sources.

**Activity:** Check a teacher-prepared response against a textbook and a trusted reference; label claims supported, unsupported or unresolved.

**Evidence of learning:** A fact-check sheet that links each corrected claim to a source.

## Stage 4 — Responsibility and Impact (Units 15–19)

### Unit 15. Bias and Missing Examples
**Learn:** Unbalanced examples and labels can produce uneven performance. Fairness means checking who or what is missed and how errors affect people.

**Activity:** Inspect a fictional dataset that overrepresents one kind of case and propose additions.

**Evidence of learning:** One identified source of bias and a targeted data improvement.

### Unit 16. Privacy, Consent and Data Minimisation
**Learn:** Personal data includes identifying details, images, voice, location and private schoolwork. Share only what is necessary and permitted.

**Activity:** Classify fictional sharing scenarios as safe, ask an adult, or do not share.

**Evidence of learning:** A practical privacy rule applied to a sample AI task.

### Unit 17. Synthetic Media and Misinformation
**Learn:** Images, voices and text can be altered or generated. A single visual clue does not prove authenticity; check source, context and independent confirmation.

**Activity:** Use teacher-selected, clearly labelled examples to practise source-checking without impersonating real students.

**Evidence of learning:** A verification checklist for suspicious content.

### Unit 18. Responsible School Use and Attribution
**Learn:** School rules, authorship, copyright and disclosure still apply. AI can assist learning but should not replace a student's explanation or misrepresent who did the work.

**Activity:** Discuss fictional homework scenarios and distinguish explanation/help from undisclosed substitution.

**Evidence of learning:** A short responsible-use agreement written in students' own words.

### Unit 19. AI Benefits, Limits and Human Roles
**Learn:** AI can support accessibility, language, research and creative work but can also exclude people, make mistakes or use resources. Some tasks are better solved without AI.

**Activity:** Compare an AI solution, a fixed-rule approach and a non-digital solution for a fictional problem.

**Evidence of learning:** A justified choice with one benefit, one risk and a human safeguard.

## Stage 5 — Apply and Design (Unit 20)

### Unit 20. Capstone: Design a Responsible AI Idea
**Learn:** A credible proposal identifies a need, input examples, likely output, difficult cases, evaluation criteria, risks and human oversight. A paper design is not a deployed AI system.

**Activity:** Teams produce a one-page design for a low-risk fictional problem, test it with at least three paper cases and explain one failure case.

**Evidence of learning:** Presentation plus a test table, privacy note and honest statement of limitations.


## Research basis

This curriculum is a proposed, AI-only learning plan—not an official CBSE syllabus. It uses the AI4K12 grade-band progression (especially Grades 6–8 and 9–12), UNESCO's student competencies across human-centred thinking, ethics, AI techniques and system design, CBSE's 2026–27 CT&AI materials as an Indian age-context reference, and Common Sense Education's grade-banded AI-literacy lessons.

## Safety and delivery

- This is AI literacy, not an electronics, robotics, or hardware curriculum. No hardware or coding prerequisite is assumed.
- Use paper-based simulations and fictional datasets wherever possible. Any live AI demo must be teacher-controlled, school-approved, and consistent with school policy.
- Do not upload students' names, faces, voices, locations, contact details, school records, passwords, or identifiable work to public AI services.
- Students should verify factual output, identify uncertainty, disclose AI assistance when required, and retain responsibility for submitted work.
- Avoid uses involving consequential decisions about real people. Use synthetic examples for bias, classification, media, and evaluation exercises.

## Assessment approach

Assess explanation and reasoning rather than tool access. Collect evidence from concept maps, paper simulations, test records, critique of AI responses, scenario discussions, and the capstone. A useful rule is: students must be able to explain the result without relying on the AI tool.

## Capstone scoring guide (10 points)

Score each criterion 0–2: (1) problem and AI fit, (2) data/examples and testing, (3) evaluation and errors, (4) privacy/fairness/human review, and (5) clarity, evidence, and honest limitations. A high score requires a reasoned design—not a claim that a paper prototype is a working AI system.
