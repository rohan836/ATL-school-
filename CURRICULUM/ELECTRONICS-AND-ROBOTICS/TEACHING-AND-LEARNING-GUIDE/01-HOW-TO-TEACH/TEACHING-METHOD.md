# How to Teach Electronics and Robotics

## 1. Teaching approach: project-first, concept-just-in-time

Avoid teaching many weeks of theory before students build anything. Start with a practical challenge, let students make a prediction, introduce the concept they need, and then have them build and test.

Use this cycle:

**Challenge → Predict → Learn → Build → Test → Improve → Explain**

- **Challenge:** Present a real-life problem or desired behavior.
- **Predict:** Ask students what they think will happen and why.
- **Learn:** Explain the small amount of theory needed for the task.
- **Build:** Students construct the circuit or prototype.
- **Test:** Observe whether it works and collect evidence.
- **Improve:** Troubleshoot or change one feature at a time.
- **Explain:** Students describe how the system works using the concepts learned.

The student should not only say, “I connected the LED.” They should be able to explain what the circuit does, why it works, and what they changed when it did not work.

## 2. Suggested 70-minute practical lesson

| Time | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0–5 min | Introduce a real-world challenge |
| 5–12 min | Students predict what will happen |
| 12–20 min | Explain the concept needed for the build |
| 20–45 min | Build the circuit or project |
| 45–55 min | Test, troubleshoot, and improve |
| 55–65 min | Demonstrate and explain |
| 65–70 min | Record observations and reflect |

Adjust the timing to the school's actual period length. Preserve meaningful build and test time.

## 3. Teaching across batches

When teaching the same class in separate C/D batches, prepare one repeatable activity and use the same learning objective, materials, and assessment criteria for each batch. Keep notes on issues encountered in the first batch so instructions or component preparation can be improved before the next batch. Do not change the learning target between batches without a reason.

## 4. Keep theory connected to a build

Examples:

- Teach voltage, current, and resistance while students investigate an LED circuit.
- Teach polarity while they test why an LED behaves differently when reversed.
- Teach series and parallel circuits by building and comparing both.
- Teach sensor values while students build an automatic light or alarm.
- Teach control logic while a sensor triggers a motor or servo.

Do not overload beginners with equations before they have observed the behavior physically. Introduce calculations when they solve a real design problem, such as choosing a suitable LED resistor.

## 5. Make troubleshooting part of learning

When a build fails, do not immediately fix it for the student. Guide them to check one thing at a time:

1. Is the power source connected and suitable?
2. Is the circuit complete?
3. Are polarity-sensitive components oriented correctly?
4. Are the breadboard rows and rails being used correctly?
5. Are the component values and connections correct?
6. Can the input and output be tested separately?
7. What changed after the last adjustment?

Encourage students to make one change at a time and observe its effect.

## 6. Classroom habits

- Demonstrate the first build clearly, then let students construct their own version.
- Use diagrams and physical components together.
- Give students roles when working in teams: builder, checker, recorder, and presenter. Rotate roles.
- Allow multiple valid designs when the challenge supports them.
- Ask students to show evidence, not only claim that a project works.
- Celebrate useful debugging and thoughtful improvements, not just a perfect first attempt.
